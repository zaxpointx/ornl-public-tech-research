# Isotopes and hot cells: how research connects to real uses

This independent public-source account is not affiliated with ORNL or DOE. It covers selected published examples, not an isotope inventory or a guide to handling radioactive material. [The evidence ledger](public-research-sources.md#isotope-and-hot-cell-batch) records each primary URL, short supporting text, retrieval date, maturity and boundaries. [KNOWN] is a direct source statement; [INFERRED] is our bounded synthesis; [UNKNOWN] means not established. Dates matter: a past demonstration, a paper's prediction and a future target are different kinds of evidence.

## Why a neutron facility and a hot cell belong in the same story

An isotope is a form of an element. Some isotopes are useful because they emit radiation or decay into another useful isotope; stable isotopes have different uses [KNOWN: I01, I06, I07]. Making an isotope and turning it into a usable product are different tasks. HFIR supports neutron-scattering research and isotope production [KNOWN: F07], while shielded hot cells allow workers to handle and process radioactive material [KNOWN: I06].

**The important distinction:** HFIR, a reactor, is not the same kind of thing as the Radioisotope Development Laboratory (RDL), which handles the particular product described below. The [facilities guide](facilities-instruments-roles.md) places HFIR's HIDRA research instrument in a separate experimental context. Their shared facility does not imply that a particular HIDRA measurement is part of an isotope-production campaign [INFERRED distinction; specific relationship UNKNOWN: F02, F07, S18].

## Story 1: old medical material, a new medical supply

The 2018 ORNL account says that radium-226 recovered from old medical devices was used as feedstock; the material was irradiated at HFIR and the resulting actinium-227 was separated and purified [KNOWN: I01]. The April 2026 RDL factsheet identifies the RDL as the place where targets are made and returned for processing after HFIR irradiation [KNOWN: S18]. This is a high-level description, not instructions for isotope production.

The relationship continues beyond ORNL: the 2018 account identifies actinium-227 as the source of radium-223, the active ingredient in Bayer's Xofigo, for a specified form of prostate cancer [KNOWN: I01]. The distinction matters: **actinium-227 is the supply material; radium-223 is the stated active ingredient**, not the same isotope. DOE's 2019 account describes the same pathway and project support [KNOWN: I02]; because it draws on the ORNL announcement, this is supporting context rather than an independent replication.

**Result and maturity:** ORNL reported an operating production arrangement in 2018, with a ten-year contract between the DOE Isotope Program and Bayer [KNOWN: I01]; the 2026 RDL factsheet says production began in 2017 [KNOWN: S18]. These are dated reports, not evidence of the contract's present terms, uninterrupted output, or a guaranteed medical supply [UNKNOWN]. ORNL later reported an improved separation approach with higher yield and lower waste, without quantifying the improvement in that brief [KNOWN: I03]. Saed Mirzadeh is identified in the 2018 ORNL story as principal investigator of that particular project; this does not establish his current role [KNOWN: I01].

## Story 2: connecting reactor measurements, modeling and isotope research

Two 2025 ORNL publication abstracts describe a californium-252 study. The first compares calculations with campaign measurements and reports results within three standard deviations of the measurements [KNOWN: I04]. Its companion compares an existing case with a **proposed**, not completed, low-enriched-uranium case and reports slightly higher calculated production rates in the modeled comparison [KNOWN: I05]. The authors' modeling result is not proof that a conversion was built or that future production rates will match the prediction [UNKNOWN].

This is an evidence-backed connection between isotope work and scientific software: the first paper names Shift and ORIGEN among its calculation codes [KNOWN: I04]. The existing [Shift / Exnihilo](../projects/shift-exnihilo.md) and [SCALE](../projects/scale.md) briefs explain these tools' public context; their controlled distribution remains out of scope. The abstracts support their use in this study, not a claim that they run every isotope campaign [UNKNOWN].

## Story 3: heat for spacecraft is a different application

NASA reports that ORNL produced a 50-gram plutonium-238 demonstration amount after a long interruption in U.S. production [KNOWN: I08; see NASA's dated 2015 account in I09]. NASA explains that radioisotope power systems convert heat from plutonium-238 decay into electricity, and describes roles for ORNL, Los Alamos and Idaho National Laboratories in the broader supply chain [KNOWN: I07]. These public sources describe a multi-laboratory effort, not an ORNL-only spacecraft power system.

A NASA report updated in 2024 describes a 0.5-kilogram shipment and a target average of 1.5 kilograms per year by 2026 [KNOWN: I10]. That target is **not** evidence that the annual rate was reached; current annual production is [UNKNOWN].

## Stable isotopes and future facilities

ORNL's facility report says the Stable Isotope Production Facility was completed in 2025 and describes xenon-129 production for lung-imaging use [KNOWN: I06]. Separately, a July 2026 ORNL report says the larger Stable Isotope Production and Research Center was under construction and planned phased operation in 2028 [KNOWN: I11]. These are distinct facilities and a dated plan, not a verified 2028 opening.

The same facility report describes a separate Radioisotope Processing Facility as planned to operate by 2039 [KNOWN: I06]. Its schedule, eventual capacity and operating status remain [UNKNOWN].

## Remaining questions and safe learning

The quantities and schedules of current isotope campaigns, the exact relationship of every listed software code to later operations, actual current output against the NASA target, and future facility completion dates remain [UNKNOWN]. This account does not assert clinical efficacy beyond the narrow published description or offer medical advice.
