# Polymer enzymes and biomanufacturing

## Plain-language summary

ORNL researchers are trying to use biology to break down nylon so its useful materials can be recovered and reused. The work combines biology, chemistry, computing, neutron science, and materials science.

## What the public record shows

The team studied enzymes that can break down nylon 66 in water. ORNL says the process may use less energy and fewer chemical inputs than traditional methods, and may make it easier to reuse the material or recover carbon fiber from composites. [S25]

The team began with 95 candidate enzymes. ORNL reports that 40% showed a natural ability to degrade nylon. Researchers then selected the strongest candidates for further testing. AI and high-performance computing helped study enzyme structure and behavior, while molecular biology and mass spectrometry were used to test the candidates. [S25]

The source also names X-ray crystallography as a way to map enzyme structure and active sites. Serena Chen is identified as a project co-lead from ORNL's Computational Sciences and Engineering Division. [S25]

## Why it connects to the atlas

This is one of the clearest examples of ORNL's cross-layer model:

- computing narrows the search;
- biology produces and tests enzymes;
- chemistry measures what happened;
- imaging reveals structure;
- materials science checks whether the result is useful for manufacturing.

The unusual connection is that an environmental recycling problem is treated as a combined computing, biology, and manufacturing problem rather than as a single chemistry experiment.

## Unknowns

- The public source does not establish a commercial recycling process.
- It does not give complete enzyme sequences, operating conditions, or performance data.
- It does not establish how much energy or cost would be saved at industrial scale.
